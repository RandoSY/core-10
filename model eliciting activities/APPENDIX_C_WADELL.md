# Appendix C — Hakon Wadell and the measurement of rock shape

This appendix accompanies Randall Young’s *How Students Define Uniformity Through Inquiry* and the CORE 10 rock lab. Use it after students propose a shape measure of their own.

## From an observed shape to a measurable claim

Hakon Adolf Wadell (1895–1962) was a Swedish geologist born in Gothenburg. In 1919, as a geology student, he and Erik Ygberg made the first recorded visit to the Grímsvötn caldera during a west-to-east crossing of Iceland’s Vatnajökull ice cap. He later worked in petroleum geology in Central America and earned a geology doctorate at the University of Chicago in 1932. A verified portrait of Wadell in later life appears on [printed page 136 of *Jökull* (PDF page 8)](https://jokull.jorfi.is/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/jokull70p129-138.pdf#page=8). The article credits J. Harlen Bretz’s 1964 memorial.

Before Wadell, C. K. Wentworth was already asking whether beach pebbles became flat through abrasion or inherited much of their form from the parent rock. Shape was evidence, but not by itself an explanation of how a pebble formed. In 1932 Wadell formalized a distinction between overall sphere-likeness and the local curvature of corners. He returned to these questions in papers on rock particles in 1933 and on quartz particles in 1935. In 1941 W. C. Krumbein described rapid methods for large sedimentary particles. The tension between an ideal geometric measure and what one can reliably measure at the bench was present from the beginning.

## Three measures that answer different questions

- **Wadell’s surface-area sphericity** compares the surface area of an equal-volume sphere with the specimen’s surface area: ψ = (36πV²)^(1/3) / A. A sphere scores 1. A smooth oblong rock scores below 1 because it has more area than an equal-volume sphere. Roundness, in Wadell’s separate sense, concerns the sharpness or curvature of corners, not the overall shape.
- **A circumscribed-sphere measure** compares the diameter of an equal-volume sphere with the diameter of the smallest sphere that encloses the specimen. This volume-based alternative does not require the rock’s surface area, but a longest caliper span is not in general the diameter of the smallest enclosing sphere.
- **The classroom caliper proxy** divides shortest by longest measured axis. It is easy to repeat but measures axial proportions, not Wadell’s area-based sphericity. A cube, measured across its three pairs of parallel faces, scores 1 on this proxy but only about 0.806 on Wadell sphericity.

## An inquiry prompt for the bench

Hand students a polished oblong rock, a ball, and a cube. Ask which two their proposed rule would tie and why. The graduated cylinder can supply a displaced volume, but it cannot supply surface area. For the tunnelled specimen, ask whether the boundary includes tunnel walls and how the method handles open passages. Use matched area and volume from the same boundary convention if computing Wadell sphericity. If only calipers are available, report the proxy by name.

Sphericity describes a **specimen**. To discuss uniformity **within a pile**, students could compare the spread of sphericity values across specimens. To discuss uniformity **within a rock**, they need a regional property and an explicit partition. These are distinct claims even when they start from the same rocks.
