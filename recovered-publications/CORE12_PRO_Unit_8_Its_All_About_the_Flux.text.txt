Instructor technical note. “Flux” is not used identically in every discipline. In radiometry, radiant flux is total radiant power in
watts, while irradiance is W/m². In heat transfer, heat flux commonly means W/m². In neutron transport, scalar neutron flux has

unifying classroom idea is still useful: transport across space and boundaries, with explicit units and geometry.

2. Lesson 8A - Heat as Measurable Energy
Illustrative calorimetry setup: water, temperature
probe, and simple insulated container.
Opening question:
How can a temperature change become a quantitative
measurement of energy?
Core relationship
Q = mcΔT
Where Q is thermal energy gained, m is mass, c is
specific heat, and ΔT is temperature change.
LAB PROTOCOL
1. Measure the mass of the water. Record instrument resolution and units.
2. Measure initial temperature T₁. Note ambient temperature as a boundary condition.
3. Add a controlled energy input or use the established pop-can calorimetry procedure.
4. Stir, measure final temperature T₂, and calculate ΔT = T₂ - T₁.
5. Compute Q = mcΔT. Repeat at least three trials when time permits.
6. Ask what the model does not yet account for: container heat capacity, evaporation, heat loss, probe lag, or
calibration.

3. Lesson 8B - Microwave Calorimetry: Watts
Become Heat
The microwave is not added as a novelty appliance. It is an energy-transfer source that lets learners infer
absorbed power from a measurable temperature rise.
Illustrative microwave-water heating setup. Use
only microwave-safe containers and normal
classroom safety practices.
WALL
ENERGY
MICROWAVE
FIELD
ABSORBED
POWER
WATER
ENERGY
HEAT
LOSS
Measured data let us test where the energy ledger closes - and where it does not.
Core equations
Q = mcΔT Pabsorbed = Q / t E = Pt 1 W = 1 J/s
Procedure
1. Use a known mass of water in a microwave-safe container.
2. Measure initial temperature and note the starting conditions.
3. Heat for a measured interval t, using a moderate time chosen to avoid boiling or superheating.
4. Stir thoroughly before taking the final temperature; microwave fields are spatially nonuniform.
5. Calculate Q = mcΔT and then P_absorbed = Q/t.
6. If a plug-in wattmeter is available, compare electrical input energy with water energy.
Important modeling caution: do not treat the microwave cavity as a uniform W/m² field. Standing-wave patterns, reflections,
container geometry, and the absorbing load matter. The robust classroom measurement is the water’s energy gain and the
inferred absorbed power.

4. Worked Example - From Temperature Rise
to Absorbed Power
Quantity Value
Water mass, m 500 g
Initial temperature, T₁ 20.0 °C
Final temperature, T₂ 40.0 °C
Heating time, t 60 s
Specific heat, c 4.186 J/(g·°C)
ΔT = 20.0 °C
Q = (500 g)(4.186 J/(g·°C))(20.0 °C) = 41,860 J
Pabsorbed = 41,860 J / 60 s ≈ 698 W
A watt is not a label on an appliance. It is a measured rate of energy transfer:
1 watt = 1 joule per second.
Student reasoning prompts
If the microwave label says 1000 W, why might the water show only about 700 W
of absorbed power?
How would doubling the water mass affect ΔT for the same absorbed energy?
Why is stirring before the final temperature reading part of the model, not merely
a procedural detail?
Which measured quantity contributes most to uncertainty in P_absorbed: mass,
temperature change, or time?

5. Lesson 8C - Energy Accounting: Where Did
It Go?
Illustrative plug-in wattmeter and water experiment. A
wall-energy measurement makes the energy ledger
experimentally testable.
The central model is deliberately simple:
Ein ≈ Estored + Elost
A residual turns “missing energy” into a scientific object:
Eresidual = Ein - (Estored + Emodeled losses)
Possible term How to test or bound it
Container heating Estimate container mass and heat capacity, or run a blank/calibration trial.
Heat loss to air Compare fast and slow heating; fit a cooling model after heating.
Evaporation Weigh before and after; avoid boiling.
Nonuniform temperature Stir; repeat measurement positions.
Instrument error Calibrate thermometer; inspect wattmeter resolution and timing.
Microwave coupling Vary container geometry or water volume and compare absorbed power.
CORE12 habit: never stop at the overlay. Inspect the residual and ask what
physical assumption the leftover contradicts.

6. Lesson 8D - Flux: Power Spread Over Area
Illustrative solar scene. Strong surface sunlight can
approach roughly 1000 W/m² under favorable conditions.
The distinction
Energy: J
Power: W = J/s
Energy flux / irradiance: W/m²
J = P/A
Flux tells us not merely how much power exists,
but how concentrated the transport is over area.
Microwave-to-sunlight comparison
If the water experiment gives Pabsorbed ≈ 698 W and strong sunlight is approximately 1000 W/m², then the
incident solar area carrying the same raw power is:
A = P/J = 698 W / (1000 W/m²) ≈ 0.70 m²
That is the bridge from a cup of water to the larger solar question. The next engineering layer is conversion
efficiency: incident solar energy is not identical to electrical output.
Flux in many disciplines

Light irradiance Eₑ or G W/m² radiant power distributed over area
Neutrons scalar flux φ m⁻²·s⁻¹ particle population × speed; reaction opportunity

7. Same Structure, New Phenomenon - The
Transport-Law Family
Many transport laws have the form: FLUX = transport property × driving
gradient
Phenomenon Law What drives the transport
Heat conduction q″ = -k∇T temperature gradient
Diffusion J = -D∇C concentration gradient
Electrical conduction J = σE electric field (voltage gradient)
Heat flux
Mass flux
Charge/current density
The mathematical similarity is not an excuse to erase the physics. Each domain has its own constitutive law,
geometry, boundary conditions, and units. CORE12 uses the repeated structure to build transfer: once
learners recognize “gradient drives flux,” they can ask what the transport coefficient means in the new
phenomenon.

8. Lesson 8E - Cooling After Heating: Energy
Leaves the System
Illustrative temperature measurement after
heating.
Microwave ON: temperature rises while energy enters
T
Microwave OFF: Newton cooling describes the return toward ambient
time
Newton cooling returns the unit to the canonical first-order dynamic form:
dT/dt = -k(T - T
a
)
This creates the complete thermal story:
MICROWAVE ON: energy in → stored thermal energy | MICROWAVE OFF: energy
out → ambient
Suggested data run
• Heat the same water sample to a reproducible starting temperature.
• Begin logging immediately after the microwave stops.
• Record T(t) at fixed intervals until the sample approaches ambient.
• Fit k and compare measured temperature with the exponential model.
• Plot the residual. Look for sensor lag, changing convection, evaporation, or nonuniformity.

10. Transfer Challenge - Neutron Flux
Without Losing the Physics
Illustrative nuclear-energy context. Reactor physics
provides a historically important example of flux
thinking.
Why include neutron flux?
It demonstrates that the “flux” idea is not confined
to heat or light. In neutron transport, flux connects
the neutron population and speed to the rate at
which nuclear interactions can occur.
A common reaction-rate form is:
R = N σ φ
where N is target number density, σ is microscopic
cross section, and φ is neutron flux.
Instructor precision note
For an advanced audience, distinguish scalar neutron flux from neutron current. Scalar flux is conventionally the integral of
angular flux over direction and has units of particles per area per time; it is closely related to neutron density times speed. Net
crossings through an oriented surface are described by current. This nuance is worth preserving because the unit’s larger lesson
is that models gain authority through explicit definitions, not slogans.
Student transfer prompt
Compare the microwave-water experiment with the neutron reaction-rate equation. In each case, what is the
transported quantity, what is the target or absorbing material, and what measured response tells you an
interaction occurred?

13. Analysis Challenges and Assessment
1. Scaling - A 250 g sample and a 500 g sample absorb the same 20 kJ. Predict which one has the larger ΔT
and by what factor.
2. Power - A sample gains 36 kJ in 45 s. What is its average absorbed power?
3. Flux - A 300 W heater spreads its output uniformly over 0.15 m². What is the average energy flux?
4. Solar bridge - At 850 W/m² incident irradiance and 22% panel efficiency, estimate electrical output from 2.0
m² of panel.
5. Residual thinking - The measured heating curve rises more slowly than the constant-power model predicts
at high temperature. Name two physical mechanisms that could produce the pattern.
6. Transfer - Explain how heat flux, current density, and diffusion flux can share a mathematical form while
representing different physical quantities.

Extension: Can We Live on Sunlight?
The unit ends exactly where the larger inquiry begins. Once a learner can move confidently among joules,
watts, watt-hours, and W/m², the questions of solar area, conversion efficiency, storage, pumping, transport,
household energy, and infrastructure become quantitatively approachable.
Students do not merely consume STEM. They produce measurements, models,
predictions, residuals, and decisions.
